RLDATIX · 2020–2021 · SENIOR PRODUCT DESIGNER

Making a stack of legacy healthcare tools feel like one product

I was one of two designers at RLDatix, working with my design manager. Our platform had grown product by product, and it showed. During the pandemic I led the hands-on work to fix that: a shared design system, a redesigned reporting entry point, and testing with the clinicians who use it.

TEAM

Two designers: me and my design manager

I OWNED

Research, interaction design, UI library, usability testing

WORKED WITH

Product, Engineering, support and clinical experts

The full risk management redesign did not ship after business priorities changed. The work produced a shared UI library, tested reporting journeys and an incremental delivery plan.

Current DCIQ interface · recreated from RLDatix’s public product visuals.

THE CHALLENGE

Every product had its own way of doing things

Navigation changed from screen to screen. Menus disappeared mid-task. Search behaved differently depending on where you were. For people working through a pandemic, that meant spending effort on the software instead of the job.

Getting lost

Similar screens and mixed navigation levels made it hard to know where you were.

Not trusting it

Unclear feedback and vanishing menus interrupted tasks and left people unsure what had happened.

Solving it twice

Without a shared foundation, teams fixed the same design problems separately.

BEFORE / AFTER

Make the next step easier to see

The goal was simple: less time working out what a screen wants you to do. Clearer hierarchy, patterns that behave the same everywhere, and status and actions shown where you need them.

01 / DESIGN SYSTEM

Decide once, reuse everywhere

With two designers, I couldn’t fix screens one by one. So I led a shared UI library for the decisions that repeat: navigation, actions, states, forms and tables. It gave designers and developers one vocabulary, so we could talk about behaviour instead of individual screens.

02 / ICON WALL

Helping people pick the right report

The Icon Wall is where staff start when they need to report an event. Icons alone don’t carry clinical concepts, so I paired every icon with a visible label and grouped events into categories to make a long list scannable. I also pulled the admin side, previously three separate flows, into one.

Understand

Report

Open the appropriate form

Configure

Manage icons and templates

Visible labels made each event understandable without relying on the icon.

Categories broke a long, configurable list into scannable groups.

One admin journey brought three separate configuration flows together.

A switch back to the old version gave established users time to adjust.

Tested with four major clients

We tested template creation with four major clients to check that local configuration didn’t make reporting harder to understand. For people used to the old version, we kept a way back to it while they adjusted.

03 / RESEARCH DURING COVID

I tested with the people who’d use it

During COVID I ran interviews and usability sessions with healthcare professionals, and worked with support, Product and clinical experts to understand what the platform could and couldn’t do. Testing shaped the work from the start rather than signing it off at the end.

My assumption

Calling “Risk trackers” “Grouped Risks” would make the feature easier to understand.

What I learned

Clinicians already knew “Risk trackers”. The supposedly simpler name added another term to learn.

What I changed

I kept the familiar term and focused on separating navigation levels and making risk actions easier to find.

What prototype testing showed

In risk management prototype testing, 96% completed tasks without facilitator help; 7% found the section dashboards distracting. The sessions also surfaced requests for printing, sharing and a personal dashboard for future iterations.

04 / DELIVERY

Getting it into the product

We planned delivery in 2–3 week sprints around technical dependencies: shared components first, then the priority journeys, checking feasibility with developers as we went. A risk-register pattern reached QA, but the full risk management redesign never shipped after the merger changed business priorities.

Prepared for implementation

Shared components, priority journeys and developer feasibility checks shaped an incremental delivery plan.

The work we had to build on

A documented UI library, a client-tested Icon Wall configuration journey and tested risk-management prototypes.

What I’d point to

The strongest part of this work was connecting platform-wide patterns to the details clinicians depended on, and knowing when a familiar term was more useful than a cleaner-sounding one.
