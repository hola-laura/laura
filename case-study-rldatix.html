RLDATIX · 2020–2021 · SENIOR PRODUCT DESIGNER

Making a stack of legacy healthcare tools feel like one product

RLDatix is a global software company helping healthcare organisations improve patient safety, manage risk and plan their workforce.

During COVID-19, healthcare teams were under intense pressure. As one of two designers at RLDatix, I worked with my design manager to make complex software easier to use. I led the shared design system, redesigned reporting and risk workflows, and tested with healthcare professionals.

TEAM

Two designers: me and my design manager

I OWNED

Research, interaction design, UI library, usability testing

WORKED WITH

Product, Engineering, support and clinical experts

THE CHALLENGE

Bringing fragmented healthcare tools together during COVID-19

Datix Cloud IQ (DCIQ) brought incident reporting, risk management and patient safety into one cloud platform. But legacy tools still had different navigation, search and feedback patterns, making everyday tasks harder to follow.

COVID-19 put healthcare teams under greater pressure. As RLDatix moved towards connected safety and risk management, our challenge was to make these workflows clearer for staff and more consistent across products.

Hard to navigate

Mixed navigation levels and inconsistent layouts made it difficult for staff to know where they were or what to do next.

Unclear feedback

Vanishing menus and unclear system responses interrupted tasks and left staff unsure whether an action had worked.

Repeated work

Without shared components, product teams solved the same interface problems separately. We needed patterns they could reuse.

BEFORE / AFTER

Make the next step easier to see

The goal was simple: less time working out what a screen wants you to do. Clearer hierarchy, patterns that behave the same everywhere, and status and actions shown where you need them.

01 / DESIGN SYSTEM

Decide once, reuse everywhere

To make our two-person design team’s work go further, I focused on shared patterns: navigation, forms, risk tables and configuration. I worked with Engineering to build a reusable UI library, so improvements could carry across products.

The system brought typography, colour, iconography and interaction states together. It gave design and engineering a common vocabulary for making consistent decisions across products.

03 / RESEARCH DURING COVID

I tested with the people who’d use it

I combined interviews and usability sessions with healthcare professionals with input from support, Product and clinical experts. This helped separate interface problems from technical constraints and test decisions before implementation.

The hypothesis

Renaming “Risk trackers” to “Grouped Risks” might make the feature easier to understand.

What testing showed

Clinicians already understood “Risk trackers”. A new label added confusion rather than clarity.

The design decision

I kept the familiar term and improved navigation hierarchy and the visibility of risk actions.

The lesson: clearer healthcare software builds on what professionals already know. Simplifying the interface doesn’t always mean changing its language.

What prototype testing showed

Prototype sessions showed where navigation and dashboard content needed work. They also surfaced requests for printing, sharing and a personal dashboard. I used these findings to refine the designs and plan later improvements.

04 / DELIVERY

Getting it into the product

I worked with Product and Engineering on an incremental delivery plan, organised into 2–3 week sprints. We prioritised shared components and the key reporting and risk journeys, checking technical feasibility as we went.

From design to delivery

The UI library and tested journeys gave developers a consistent foundation for implementation.

Used by customers

The redesigned risk-management screens went into day-to-day use with customers.

What I’d point to

A consistent platform needed more than visual polish: shared component behaviour, practical local configuration and terminology clinicians already understood.
