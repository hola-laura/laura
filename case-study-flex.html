The product

Flex was more than buy now, pay later.

Monzo is a UK digital bank with over 10 million customers. In 2021 it launched Flex, a hybrid of buy now, pay later and a credit card: one credit limit, with an instalment plan for each purchase.

  • Not tied to a retailerKlarna-style plans depend on the shop. Flex worked wherever you could pay by card.
  • Move it afterwardsCustomers could move a purchase from their current account to Flex after paying.
  • A finish line for every purchaseEach plan had a clear end date, so people could see their debt going down.

By early 2023 Flex was 15% behind its customer goal. It needed a new position, and an experience that could live up to it.

I joined full time in February 2023 to land that vision in five months. I covered both squads, Growth and Core, working with a researcher, marketing, legal and compliance.

What came next

Once Flex worked as a card, two harder questions followed.

What about the things a card can’t pay for? And how do you change someone’s credit limit without losing their trust?

Money Transfers

Paying for what a card can’t.

Builders, personal trainers, group holidays. About 1 in 5 customers who gave feedback wanted this, and Flex needed new revenue that also helped them.

What we learned
People expected it where they already pay others, and a clear fee felt better than interest.
What we did
We built Flex into Monzo’s payment flow instead of a separate feature. When testing showed some people missed the fee, we added a deliberate step the first time.
What happened
Adoption went well beyond the forecast.
Limit management

A limit change should never feel like a surprise.

It’s one of the highest-trust moments in credit. Raise a limit carelessly and people can borrow more than they should. Cut it carelessly and it feels like the bank is taking something away.

What we decided
No surprises: fair notice, a plain reason and a real choice, every time.
Raising a limit
An offer people could turn down or opt out of, with a moment to think before borrowing more.
Reducing a limit
A process with warning and a choice. If someone started using Flex again, the reduction cancelled itself.

01The test that won more applications but fewer accounts.

Our survey pointed to two ways to make Flex more relevant: many customers used credit cards, and many spent money travelling. So we tested a message for each. The travel message got more people to start an application, but fewer ended up with an account. Interest isn’t the same as customers, so we chose the credit card message.

02Why the kindest default turned out to be the riskiest.

As people used Flex more often, choosing a plan for every purchase became tiring, and some simply forgot. So we let them set a default. Paying in full is interest free, so it looked like the safest option to put first. But when the whole bill arrived, some people didn’t have enough in their account, and more payments failed. So we recommended stopping that version and kept testing a safer one: “Choose for every purchase” shown first, so people still made the decision themselves. With that order, failed payments barely changed.

03The maths hiding behind one slider.

Customers with many purchases wanted to choose one monthly amount, without editing each purchase. It sounds simple, but under the balance every purchase is its own small loan, with its own term and interest. So moving one slider means working out how to split the money across all of them, in the way that saves the customer the most interest.

04What I’d do differently now.

I’d bring the business and risk teams in from the first workshop. Our hardest decisions weren’t only about design: a repayment option could be easy to use, yet cost Monzo money or put customers at risk. Talking about that from day one makes those decisions easier. I’d also measure success by whether customers stay on top of their payments, as well as how many sign up.
