01 / Reframe the promise

The product said “pay later”. Customers already used credit cards.

Acquisition was 15% behind plan. In a survey of 1,924 eligible customers, 65% already used credit cards and 9% used BNPL. I used that gap with Marketing to make Flex’s credit-card proposition clearer.

We made the price explicit: 0% interest when paying in full or over three monthly payments; 24% APR over six or twelve instalments, with no hidden fees and fee-free overseas use.

The in-app framing test lifted account creation by 7.1%. In a separate awareness test, it moved from 0.65% to 0.77%, reported as a 20% relative uplift.

The awareness test covered 869,807 people: 429,853 control and 439,954 message recipients.

02 / Bring the account together

One balance. A clearer starting point.

37% of Flex customers had more than ten active purchases. Paying them off one by one was hard work.

The account mock brought the virtual card, total balance (£401.80), available limit (£614.20), next payments, activity and settings together, with quick actions to pay early, open card details or pay the whole balance.

9/10participants completed tasks unaided in the second usability round.
03 / Make repayments understandable

Show what each choice changes.

Customers could choose to pay in full, pay the monthly minimum, or choose for each purchase. If an extra payment would not reduce the next scheduled payment, the flow said so before confirmation.

The balance left to pay and interest saving were made visible. Monthly-payment editing followed later, with future payments and interest shown before a change.

The point of friction was the moment where a clear consequence could prevent a costly misunderstanding.

I worked with Legal and Compliance. The project record does not document screen-reader testing or research specifically with financially vulnerable customers; both would be explicit follow-ups, alongside the contrast audit.

Phased after the first release

Change this month. See the months ahead.

Engineering could not combine monthly-amount payments with future-payment editing without complex allocation changes. We phased the journeys; the later edit view showed the effect on upcoming payments and interest.

04 / Make the system shippable

One account view; deeper controls phased in.

I aligned the V1 around a useful shared account, clearer repayment defaults and simple payment actions. More complex monthly-payment editing came later because of allocation constraints.

The work used three repeatable interaction patterns: an account summary connecting balance and next payment, a repayment-default choice, and a consequence explanation before confirmation. These were patterns in the Flex journeys; this project did not include a documented contribution to Monzo’s shared design-system library.

05 / Lead across teams

One customer promise. Shared decisions across Growth and Core.

With two squads, Marketing, Data, Engineering, Legal and Compliance, I kept decisions anchored in customer value, business impact and engineering effort. The survey supported the credit-card framing; rollout used 50k-customer cohorts.

IN HINDSIGHT

Bring allocation edge cases forward

I would map payment-allocation constraints with Engineering earlier, before detailed flow design.

NEXT HYPOTHESIS

Can customers predict the impact?

After a 50k cohort, I would test whether customers can predict how a repayment change affects the next payment and total interest before expanding the controls.
