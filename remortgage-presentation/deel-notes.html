Remortgaging at Monzo, 2025–26

starts at 0:00 · about 0:34

Hi, I'm Laura. Thanks for having me. I'm going to present a remortgaging case study from Monzo, where I was a Senior Product Designer.

Let me start with the ending. We beat our referral goal by 75 percent, but mortgages didn't grow at the same pace.

So most of what you'll see is how I found out why, and what I designed to fix it. First, a bit of context on the product.

2

Monzo customers loved seeing their mortgage in the app, but it didn't make money

starts at 0:34 · about 0:35

When I joined, Homeownership was basically this. You could connect your mortgage, from almost any UK lender, and see it in the Monzo app.

Your balance, how much of your home you actually own, what happens if you overpay, and how a new rate would change what you pay every month.

There were 250,000 mortgages connected, and customers loved it. But it was good for engagement, and that was it: it didn't make any money.

3

To make money, we launched remortgaging with Better.co.uk. It worked, but not enough

starts at 1:09 · about 2:05

Remortgaging was the obvious place to start making money.

Quick context on the UK: most mortgages have a fixed deal for a few years. When it ends, your rate goes up, so people switch to a new deal. That's remortgaging.

And we often knew when that moment was coming. In onboarding, we ask about your deal end, and about your home to work out your equity.

It's not the focus of this case study, but putting these questions in onboarding was my idea. They already existed in the product, but very few people answered them. By January 2025, we'd gone from 38% to 88% of new customers giving us their deal-end date.

So we partnered with Better.co.uk, a mortgage broker. A broker finds you a deal and does the paperwork for you. When the mortgage completes, the lender pays the broker, the customer pays nothing, and Monzo gets a share if the customer started in our app.

Every customer we send to a partner is a referral. I designed that first journey. It was a click-out: people left Monzo and finished on Better's website.

It proved there was a business: around 5,000 customers over time. But our goal for the first six months was about 300 applications a month, around a million pounds a year. By September 2024 we had 70, and growth stopped.

And once people left the app, we only saw the totals Better sent us, like how many dropped out at the form. Not why, and we couldn't test anything on their side. That was my big learning: our success ended at the referral.

5

We lent our engineers for two months, and used the time to listen

starts at 4:32 · about 1:02

The reset started in a way I didn't expect.

In February 2025, another team at Monzo, Financial Health, needed engineers. They had a real business need, and Homeownership wasn't going the way we wanted.

We agreed they could borrow our engineers for two months, and we decided to use that time well. We ran a discovery phase, to understand why our work wasn't moving the numbers.

6

Customers asked us for four things

starts at 5:34 · about 1:35

Customers told us four things.

The first one was, don't send me out. People expected to get their new deal inside Monzo, they didn't expect to end up on another website.

The second thing was, use what you already know about me, don't ask me again. And it's true that from sign-up, onboarding or other products we already know a lot, like your income, your mortgage balance or your address.

The third was, let me choose how I interact. With our partner at the time, Better.co.uk, everything started with a phone call, and they called customers on their own schedule, whenever they could.

Some people wanted a phone call, because it made them feel safer. But other people preferred less intrusive ways, like chat or email, at a time they chose. They wanted to do it on their own, knowing there was a human expert there if they needed one.

And the fourth one was, tell me what's happening, because the waiting was the most stressful part for them. A remortgage usually takes between four and six months to complete. So it was quite long, sometimes customers didn't know what was happening, and they didn't have a place to come back to and check.

7

The whole remortgage, without leaving Monzo

starts at 7:09 · about 3:01

I turned all of that into a design vision: the whole remortgage, inside Monzo. What you see playing here comes straight from my Figma frames.

The application comes into the app. You see the benefits of remortgaging with us, and the deals you could get, because we already have some data about you.

Then you choose how to start. Talk to an adviser first, for a more personal experience. Or see the recommendations first, and talk to an adviser after.

To get a mortgage you have to fill in a fact-find, a very thorough affordability assessment. Here it comes pre-filled with what Monzo already knows. You can review it, but most of the form is already done.

You see who your adviser is, and you choose how to talk to them. And when your recommendation arrives, it comes with a note from your adviser, and their expert recommendations.

You can follow your progress in the app, and see if there's anything you need to do.

And you upload all your documents from the app. Something I find really interesting in the vision: we proposed the first B2C website experience, so people could also upload documents from a laptop. Research showed that many of these documents live on a computer, not on a phone.

And at the end, you get your new mortgage, you connect it to Monzo, and the homeownership journey starts again, until it's time to remortgage.

On this slide I've also attached an article about our vision work, written by our product director.

I should say, a vision is a direction for the team. We weren't promising to build everything. But it made one thing very clear: to give customers the experience they wanted, we needed to own much more of the journey than our referral partner allowed us to.

At that point, the business was also talking about bringing in more partners. We didn't know we were going to buy Habito. But from the research and the early vision work, we knew we needed to offer other ways to communicate, and more flexibility, closer to how Monzo works.

So the vision became part of the argument for changing our partnership model. Later Monzo bought Habito, and that's what made it possible to build. I wasn't part of the acquisition decision, but the team still goes back to this vision.

9

Reach people before they're ready to remortgage

starts at 13:00 · about 2:50

OK, reach. We knew where we wanted the product to go, but we couldn't build the vision overnight. We still had revenue targets, and we depended on what our broker could do. So we worked on two tracks: reach more people now, and move more of the journey into Monzo, step by step.

From the second half of 2025, reach was the part we could control. Our hypothesis was simple. We weren't growing because we couldn't reach enough people.

Most people who were about to remortgage hadn't connected their mortgage, so we couldn't see them. At the time, about 365,000 customers had connected one, and more than two million had one we couldn't see.

Honestly, I always thought that asking people to connect first was a barrier. And here I could do something about it.

What if we gave them a tool to see mortgage rates? From there they could connect. And we could capture their deal-end date, to reach them at the right moment, even if they never connected. That's the first screen.

That's the second screen. We didn't know their home, but we knew where they lived, from their Monzo address. So we guessed the filters, like the property value and the mortgage balance, from averages for their area. You saw rates for a home like yours without typing anything, and you could change them to your own numbers.

A bit later, we launched estimated deals for connected customers. That's the last screen: deals from real lenders, with the monthly repayment and the fees. They're estimated because they come before the fact-find and before you apply.

We also ran rate alerts and new entry points across the app. I'm happy to go into those later.

For the result. Between late 2025 and spring 2026, all our referrals together beat our referral goal by about 75 percent. That's around 7,500 referrals, when the goal was 4,300. New mortgage connections went from about 200 to about 1,000 a day. And rate alerts were behind 44 percent of recent referrals.

So the hypothesis was right. But keep one thing in mind for later. A referral is only the first step. It isn't a mortgage yet.

10

I designed the onboarding to Habito

starts at 15:49 · about 2:38

And then we started with Habito. At the end of 2025 Monzo agreed to buy Habito, which is another mortgage broker. From December we sent customers to both partners, Better and Habito, and in October 2026 we stopped sending them to Better.

With Better we already had entry points and an onboarding. We didn't have time to redesign and test the whole onboarding, so I translated Better's onboarding to Habito. I applied what we had learned, and balanced the trade-offs that came with Habito.

Of course, we had to adapt some things to how Habito worked operationally.

Learning number one: people thought we were a comparison site. We led with 'compare remortgage deals from over 100 lenders', so when a broker started asking about their finances, it felt like a trick.

So I reframed it, and redesigned the first screen to lead with the help of a mortgage expert: 'Find a mortgage with free expert help'. It went live in May 2026, and Ops told us they were getting fewer questions about comparing deals. I don't have an exact number for you, though.

We also tweaked the wording to mention the acquisition. At first we couldn't say Habito by Monzo publicly, so we just had the Habito logo, without 'by Monzo'. Later we could, and to be honest, we tested the copy quite a lot.

Learning number two: people didn't want to leave Monzo. We couldn't avoid sending them to Habito, or to another partner, yet. You had to create a Habito account from the Monzo app, so to make that feel less negative, I proposed framing the account as a benefit: you can check your progress here, in the app, or on Habito's website, wherever suits you.

Then another operational trade-off: Habito needed to know if you were remortgaging with someone else, to add them as a second applicant. We were working on moving those questions into the application form, out of the onboarding, but that was going to take a bit longer.

And from there, we took you to Habito.

We shipped the simplest tracker first

starts at 19:35 · about 2:52

I'm going to talk a bit about the first progress tracker. I want to show you how it evolved, because the trade-offs say more than the final screen.

The first iteration was, to me, the ideal one at that point. Everything in one place: your steps, the deals you got, and your expert. It answered 'tell me what's happening' best, and you didn't have to leave the screen to know where you were.

But it didn't work for us, because at that point it depended on data we couldn't get. When we sat down with engineering, not every step Habito gave us could be tracked on our side, and there were bits of data we just couldn't get yet.

So with what I learned from engineering, I made a second proposal, with only the steps we could track accurately. One screen per state, with what's done and what's next. But from an engineering point of view, we needed a new screen for every state, and it was very slow to build at that time.

So we shipped the third one: Habito's own steps, with a link to their website at the exact step each customer was on. We chose it because it was the one we could launch on time, and we saved the ideal version for later.

Customers found it useful, but it didn't move the metrics. And that makes sense, because a tracker without the rest of the journey doesn't do much more than reduce the stress of waiting. But it was a point in favour of our vision.

On the tech side, it was a very hard project. Our engineers had to build a lot of bridges and APIs so the tracker was accurate. At the beginning, the data even reached us two days late, while Habito was already showing it, so the first version didn't have parity. We had a few complaints about that, but we worked with Habito to fix it, and now the app has full parity.

This is a decision I'd make again: ship small. It moved us closer to the vision, and it showed us early how hard the data part was.

Referrals beat the plan, the later stages didn’t

starts at 22:26 · about 1:35

Back to the numbers. With Habito, we were still sending a lot of people.

This chart shows each step of the Habito funnel against its 2026 target.

Referrals were at 153 percent of the target. But from referral to recommendation, we were at 72 percent. And from recommendation to sending the application to the lender, only 44 percent.

So the biggest drop was after the recommendation. People got a deal from their adviser, and then they didn't go ahead.

And that's when they should be most motivated. They had filled in the whole form, the fact-find, and talked to an adviser. So it wasn't only about the quality of the traffic.

We only make money when the mortgage completes, and that takes months. So we picked an earlier signal: accepted recommendations. When you accept the deal, that's the first moment you really commit.

It was the same problem we had with Better. The difference was that now we could see where it happened, and we could change it.

For me, this is the turning point. The question wasn't 'how do we send more people?' anymore. It was 'why don't people with a recommendation go ahead?'

So I went to find out, with the team.

14

Where people got stuck, and who could fix it

starts at 24:01 · about 2:02

With our PM I co-led a workshop about the drop-off, and then I turned what we learned into this. It's called an opportunity solution tree.

Basically you have one goal at the top, the causes under it, and the ideas under each cause. At the top we have one outcome, more recommendations accepted. Under that, the problem.

Our hypothesis was that these customers really want to remortgage, but when they see the recommendation, they don't feel confident enough to act.

Under that, five areas. They're all on the slide, so I'll talk about three. First, relevance. The recommendation feels generic. There's no reasoning and no human behind it.

Second, the experience break. People get sent out of Monzo. One customer said it felt like being palmed off to another organisation.

Third, momentum. People were ready, but our follow-up was slow and easy to miss.

For me, the most useful part is the last row. For each area I asked two questions. Is this a root cause, or does it just make things worse? And can Monzo fix it, or is it in Habito's hands?

Relevance, for example, was mostly in Habito's hands, because they write the recommendation.

And these were hypotheses. We hadn't proven any of them. Rates, eligibility or how fast the adviser replied could explain part of the drop too, and most of that was on Habito's side. The bets were how we tested the part we could change.

That showed us where design in Monzo could make the biggest difference. The four bets I'll show you next come straight from here.

I treated every decision as a bet

16

Move the application into Monzo, pre-filled

starts at 27:16 · about 2:17

The second bet is also about getting people to a recommendation. The application form, which was the most fragmented part of the whole journey. It was outside Monzo, on the broker's website, and it was long. Around 200 questions.

With Better, in 2025, almost half of the people dropped out there. That number came from Better, by the way, because we couldn't see that part of the journey ourselves.

And with Habito it was still on a separate website.

I designed the application inside Monzo, and there are two decisions I care about.

The second is saving your progress, so you can come back later, because nobody has all their mortgage details ready in one go. That one is in the next version.

And we measure it step by step, so the team can see exactly where people stop. On the right you can see two versions.

The first one is a simple form, and it went live at the end of September. We tested it with half of our customers. People on the team tell me the first signal is strong.

About 40 percent more people are finishing the form and getting to a recommendation, compared with when they started in Monzo and had to go to Habito's website.

The data isn't mature yet, so for me it's still an early signal. And the next version is designed, on the right: it tells you what you'll need and how long it takes, shows what we've already answered for you, and breaks the form into five sections that keep your progress.

A push the moment the recommendation is ready

starts at 29:33 · about 2:13

The next two bets are about the moment people decide. And the first thing we did there was the quickest one. If you remember the tree, people were ready, but our follow-up was slow.

At that point the only reminder was an email from Habito.

The first experiment was very simple. Send a push from Monzo on day zero, the moment the recommendation is ready, which is when people care the most, and again on day three. The design question was where that push should take you.

I explored two options. Option A was a short screen in the middle. It tells you your recommendation is ready, it shows that Habito is regulated and that it's free, and then it takes you there.

Two taps and a whole screen to build trust, but we had to build it. Option B opened the progress tracker, which already existed, with the button on the current step.

Three taps and more distraction, but nothing new to build. On paper I preferred A, because it was the cleaner test.

But the tracker already existed, so with B we could test much faster, and that's what launched in May. And I think it was the right decision, because we needed to learn fast.

We measured it on accepted recommendations, and they went up. I don't remember the exact number, so I'm not going to give you one. And I think I know why it worked.

What customers needed to see before choosing a mortgage

starts at 33:38 · about 2:38

So once we saw that the view-only version wasn't enough, I started redesigning it. At the top of my file I wrote the goal.

Help people decide with confidence when they're ready, or wait on purpose, without dropping off. It took me four rounds in about four months, and every round fixed something the previous one was missing.

The first version was in June 2026. It's presented through the adviser, it says 'Will's top pick for you'. When you see the name of a real person, you trust it more.

And the alternatives are next to the top pick, so it doesn't feel like we're pushing you.

Then I reviewed my own design against the research and I wrote down what was missing. You couldn't contact the adviser from that screen, and there was no honest sense of urgency.

The second round was low fidelity. It showed the total cost first, before the monthly rate, and it compared staying with your lender and switching, in pounds. The third one went to a design crit on the 17th of September. It was a hub with tools, for example to stress-test the deal. And the feedback was that too many things were competing for attention.

In 2019, in a first remortgage test with the broker Mojo, deals were ranked by their true cost, and customers thought they weren't competitive. When people aren’t ready, or the lender withdraws the rate

starts at 37:38 · about 1:22

For me the part I'm most proud of is what happens when things don't go to plan. There are two cases.

The first one is when I'm interested, but I'm not ready.

Today you can stop your application at any time, but if you come back, you have to start again from zero. And if the screen only has accept and reject, you lose all of those people.

The adviser chat: a first step in the app

starts at 40:22 · about 2:48

OK, the adviser. This bet is about relevance, and about choice. The vision said people should choose how they talk to an adviser.

But in reality Habito's advisers worked by chat, in a separate tool, and at that time the chat opened in Habito, outside Monzo.

On the left is the first step we launched, and I designed it. Habito's advisers chat on Intercom, so for now you open Habito's chat inside the app, from the tracker. I know it's not the perfect experience. But people don't get confused by a chat on a website, and they don't have to leave Monzo.

On the right is the full design. We had two options. Wait about nine months for Monzo's own chat, or put Habito's chat inside the app in about a month. I proposed the fast one, because every month we waited we were losing people at the decision.

The extra cost was small, about 43 thousand dollars a year, and the product review estimated it would pay for itself in about a month.

But having a second chat inside Monzo is risky. People could ask their adviser about a blocked card, or ask Monzo support about their mortgage.

So I designed three guardrails. Gate: we only show it during an active remortgage. Signpost: the first time you open the chat, a short intro shows who you're talking to, a real person and a mortgage expert, and when they'll reply. And Recover: if you end up in the wrong place, one tap gets you to the right one.

We also agreed a kill switch before starting, so a limit that tells you in advance when to stop. Above 2 percent of chats in the wrong place we investigate, and above 5 percent we roll back.

And in the end we paused it. People wanted it, and we could build it. But in 2026 first-time buyers became the team's first goal. That was a business decision: we needed to show that buying Habito had been a good decision, and most Monzo customers are first-time buyers.

The goal is still the adviser inside Monzo. You can see the whole flow in the appendix, if you want.

Where AI helps me, and where it isn’t worth it

starts at 45:56 · about 2:34

About AI in general: it didn't decide the direction of this work, the research did. It helped me with speed and with risk.

So I use AI in four ways. One, to experiment, like this checker, or a custom AI assistant I built with our product context.

Two, built into how the team works, checking our UI copy before it gets to Compliance.

Three, for my own work. Drafting copy and scenarios, trying quick UI variations, and analysing support conversations. That's how I found out people were disconnecting their mortgage because the data was wrong.

And four, to share what I learn. The lunch and learn, the feedback channel, and a prompt library for the team.

And there are places where I've decided it isn't worth it. Basically, when it adds a layer of complexity without improving a decision or an outcome.

I don't use it to decide the strategy. It can sort signals and suggest hypotheses, but it doesn't replace understanding the context, negotiating priorities or owning a hard decision.

I don't use it to pretend we did research. It can summarise interviews, but it doesn't replace talking to people, and seeing the difference between what they say and what they do.

And no high-impact decisions without a human. In financial products, I wouldn't let an opaque recommendation affect someone's eligibility, risk or access without an explanation, oversight and a way to review it.

I also don't use it to generate solutions before we understand the problem, or to produce generic designs or copy in bulk. If the result needs so much review that it's faster to do it well from the start, it isn't saving any work. And it doesn't fix a team that isn't aligned.

So I use it to take away mechanical work, and the judgment and the accountability stay with the team. The way I'd put it: I use AI to increase the speed and breadth of exploration, but I don't delegate product judgment or accountability to it.

The home screen, in eleven states

Appendix · about 1:12

My audit of the product pointed at one place to start: the Focus View. It's the home screen for your mortgage, the room every other journey opens into. And it had one design for everyone.

So my proposal was a screen that adapts: what you see depends on where you are in your mortgage. Watch it change.

A3

Four decisions, and why I made them

Appendix · about 1:11

Four decisions inside it that I'd defend. One: value without connecting. Millions of customers haven't connected a mortgage, and before this they had no home here at all.

If you only looked at rates, your saved rates are the first thing you see when you come back. Your earlier visit counts. Two: make the cost concrete.

Not 'your deal ends soon', but what you pay now and what you'll pay if you do nothing. That's urgency the customer can check. Three: the remortgage tracker lives in the home screen, not on a separate one.

The home is the hub for everything about your mortgage. And four: never a dead end. If the data doesn't load, we show the last numbers we had, with the date.

And the whole thing is resolved each time the screen opens, with three questions: are you connected, are you remortgaging, and how many months until your deal ends.

A4

Four ways to show two mortgages on one home

Appendix · about 1:06

This is an edge case that turned out to be a foundation. Our tools were built for one person with one mortgage.

But people have two mortgages on one home, or a couple shares one, and then the numbers got confusing: one deal-end date for two products, or two partners seeing a different value for the same house.

For our UX days I explored four ways to show it: a property overview, stacked sections, a switcher, and combining both into one deal, which could even save money. The useful part was the insight underneath.

The data already added up at the level of the property. The screen didn't.

So the answer to every case was the same: show the property first, then its mortgages. And I'd start with the cheapest fix: the case where both mortgages were already on screen and simply rendered badly.
