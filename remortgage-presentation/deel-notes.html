1

Remortgaging at Monzo, 2025–26

Hello, I'm Laura. Thanks for having me, and for taking the time to learn about me and this project.

I'm going to present a remortgaging case study from Monzo, where I was a Senior Product Designer. It covers 2025 and 2026.

I worked at Monzo for over five years, and for the last two I focused on their Homeownership product.

The team was one PM, an engineering manager, six engineers, a data scientist and a user researcher, and we worked with Ops, Compliance and a broker called Habito.

My role covered setting the vision, turning that vision into the product, UX and UI, experiments, and AI tooling.

Let's start.

2

Monzo customers loved seeing their mortgage in the app, but it didn't make money

When I joined, Homeownership was basically this. You could connect your mortgage, from almost any UK lender, and see it in the Monzo app.

Your balance, how much of your home you actually own, what happens if you overpay, and how a new rate would change what you pay every month.

There were 250,000 mortgages connected, and customers loved it. But it was good for engagement, and that was it: it didn't make any money.

3

To make money, we launched remortgaging with Better.co.uk. It worked, but not enough

We already had connected customers, and we knew when their deal was ending, because we asked in onboarding. So remortgaging was the safest place to start making money.

In summer 2024, we partnered with a mortgage broker, Better.co.uk. The broker finds the deal and does the paperwork. When the mortgage completes, the lender pays the broker, the customer pays nothing, and Monzo gets a share.

Every customer we send to a partner is a referral. I designed this first journey. It was a click-out: people finished on Better's website.

It proved there was a business: by the end of 2025, we estimate that more than a thousand customers had completed a new mortgage through Monzo's remortgage journey. But our target was £1 million a year by September 2024. We measured it as an annualised run-rate: one month's revenue, times twelve. That meant around 300 applications a month. We expected about 80 percent to complete their mortgage, so about 240 completions a month, at roughly £350 each. When September came, we had about 70 applications a month. We missed the target by a long way. So the team lowered it to about £630,000 a year by March 2025.

And because we didn't own the rest of the journey, we could only try to grow the top of the funnel.

4

In 2025 and 2026, I led design for remortgaging through a strategy reset and an acquisition

This is the part I want to focus on in this case study: 2025 and 2026, when we went through two big changes. First we reset the strategy, and then there was an acquisition.

In 2025 I led the vision, which took a couple of months. I'll tell you about it in a second.

Then came Habito, a mortgage broker that has been part of the Monzo group since April 2026. We, the team at Monzo, knew about it a bit earlier. From late 2025, I designed how its journey came into our app. The second screen you see is from that.

Let's start with how the strategy changed.

5

We lent our engineers for two months, and used the time to listen

The reset started in a way I didn't expect.

Early in 2025, our growth was slowing. We were launching lots of experiments, but they weren't moving the numbers. So in February we decided to pause engineering on Homeownership for two months.

From March, our engineers helped two other teams, Financial Health and Payments. And we used that time to run a discovery phase, to understand what wasn't working.

We interviewed two groups of people. One group was people who had remortgaged with us, and the other was people who had started and given up.

Our product director wrote about this on the Monzo blog, and the article is linked on this slide. Anyway, that phase is what changed the strategy.

6

Customers asked us for four things

Customers told us four things.

First: don't send me out. People expected to get their new deal inside Monzo. They didn't expect to end up on another website.

Second: use what you already know about me, don't ask me again. And it's true: from sign-up, onboarding and the other products people use, we already know a lot, so we should ask them for less.

Third: let me choose how I interact. With our partner Better, everything started with a phone call, and they called customers on their own schedule, whenever they could.

Some people appreciated the phone call, because it made them feel safer. But other people preferred less intrusive ways, like chat or email, at a time they chose.

And fourth: tell me what's happening, because the waiting was the most stressful part for them. The full customer journey usually took four to six months, from first exploring options to completion. The formal application was only one part of that journey, and could take weeks. It was a long time. Customers sometimes didn't know what was happening, and they had no place to come back to and check.

7

The whole remortgage, without leaving Monzo

I turned all of that into a design vision: the whole remortgage, inside Monzo.

The application comes into the app. You see the benefits of remortgaging with us, and the deals you could get, because we already have some data about you.

Then you choose how to start. Talk to a mortgage expert first, for a more personal experience. Or see the recommendations first, and talk to a mortgage expert after.

To get a mortgage you have to fill in a fact-find, a very thorough affordability assessment. Here it comes pre-filled with what Monzo already knows. You can review it, but most of the form is already done.

You see who your mortgage expert is, and you choose how to talk to them. And when your recommendation arrives, it comes with a note from your mortgage expert explaining their advice.

You can follow your progress in the app, and see if there's anything you need to do.

And you upload all your documents from the app. Something I find really interesting in the vision: we proposed the first B2C website experience, so people could also upload documents from a laptop. Research showed that many of these documents live on a computer, not on a phone. It stayed as a proposal in the vision: we never built it.

And at the end, you get your new mortgage, you connect it to Monzo, and the homeownership journey starts again, until it's time to remortgage.

We presented the vision to leadership in May 2025, after about two months of work.

I should say, a vision is a direction for the team. We weren't promising to build everything. But it made one thing very clear: to give customers the experience they wanted, we needed to own much more of the journey than our referral partner allowed us to.

We also changed how we measured success. Instead of stopping at the referral, we started measuring conversion further down the funnel.

At that point, the business was also talking about bringing in more partners. We didn't know Monzo was going to buy Habito. But from the research and the early vision work, we knew we needed to offer other ways to communicate, and more flexibility, closer to how Monzo works.

So the vision became part of the argument for changing our partnership model. Later Monzo bought Habito, and that's what made it possible to build. I wasn't part of the acquisition decision, but the team still goes back to this vision.

8

I turned the vision into a plan the team could ship

A vision doesn't help if the team can't build it. So, with the support of our engineering manager, I ran a session with the team to turn it into a plan. At that point, our partner was still Better.

The plan had to fit the team's goals for April to September 2025. We had missed both earlier targets, so the team set a new one for this period. The first goal was revenue: from about £278,000 a year, based on January's completions, to £990,000 a year by the end of September, both as annualised run-rates. With the same assumptions, that meant about 240 completions and close to 300 applications a month. We didn't reach that one either, although by October we were at about £916,000. The second was getting more customers who were about to remortgage to use Homeownership. We also had to look after the platform's technical health, and assess moving from Better to Habito.

Now was what we could start straight away. Opening the door to people without a connected mortgage, because we were behind on revenue and needed more people. The progress tracker, which for me is the most valuable idea: while we were still sending people out, they could always see where they were and what came next. And pre-fill, so people don't type things twice.

Next needed more work: revisiting the onboarding, which was the first thing we built with Habito, our own application form, estimated deals, and a first recommendation in Monzo that you could see but not accept.

With Better, we also tested letting people choose: talk to a mortgage expert first, or see the deals first. It tested very well, but Habito couldn't support it yet, so we parked it, and kept estimated deals as the simpler way to see deals first.

Later had the big pieces: accepting the recommendation in Monzo, the chat with your mortgage expert, and document upload. In 2026, Monzo's own chat and the document upload moved back again, because the focus moved to first-time buyers. As a step in between, we launched Habito's chat inside the app. I'll come back to that.

9

Reach people before they're ready to remortgage

OK, reach.

We couldn't build the vision overnight. We still had revenue targets, and we depended on what our broker could do. So we worked on two tracks: reach more people now, and move the journey into Monzo, step by step.

Reach was the part we could control. Our hypothesis was simple: we weren't growing because we couldn't reach enough people. Most people about to remortgage hadn't connected their mortgage, so we couldn't see them. About 365,000 customers had connected one. More than two million had one we couldn't see.

I always thought that asking people to connect first was a barrier. So what if we gave everyone a tool to see mortgage rates?

From there, they could connect. And we could get their deal end, to reach them at the right moment, even if they never connected. That's the first screen.

On the second screen, we filled in the filters, like the property value and the mortgage balance, with averages for their area, based on their Monzo address. So they could see rates for a home like theirs without typing anything. And of course, they could change them.

We were already thinking about indicative rates while we worked on the vision. By April 2025, the version I designed was live for connected customers, because it was easier: we already had their data. It had the rate chart and rate alerts, so people got a notification when rates changed. Later, we made it work for people without a connected mortgage too. That was the full product.

In our May 2025 review, rate alerts were linked to about 44 percent of recent referrals. That was an internal, approximate read, not proof that the alerts caused all of them.

The last screen is estimated deals, for connected customers. These are deals from real lenders, with the monthly repayment and the fees. They're estimated because they come before you apply.

I also designed entry points across the app, so people could find all this more easily.

And a few more things I designed and the team built. Some people skipped the deal-end question in onboarding. So I designed a header in Homeownership that asks them to finish setting up. That took us to 91% of people giving us their deal end, up from 88%.

And at onboarding, if your deal ended in the next six months, we took you straight to remortgaging. You can see examples behind the button.

The impact: by early 2026, total referrals across all our partners were around 7,500 a month, against a target of 4,300 a month. That's about 75 percent above plan.

And new mortgage connections went from about 200 to about 1,000 a day.

The results supported our reach hypothesis. They also showed that referrals were only the first step: we still needed to improve what happened after someone reached the broker.

Keep that in mind for later: a referral isn't a mortgage yet. We only got paid when the customer accepted the recommendation and completed their new mortgage.

10

I designed the onboarding to Habito

And then we started with Habito.

At the end of 2025 Monzo agreed to buy Habito, which is another mortgage broker. From December we sent customers to both partners, Better and Habito, and in October 2026 we stopped sending them to Better.

With Better we already had entry points and an onboarding designed. We didn't have time to redesign and test a whole onboarding, so I translated Better's onboarding to Habito. I applied what we had learned, and balanced the trade-offs that came with Habito.

Of course, we had to adapt some things to how Habito worked operationally.

Learning number one: people thought we were a comparison site. We led with 'compare remortgage deals from over 100 lenders', so when a broker started asking about their finances, it felt like a trick.

So I reframed it, and redesigned the first screen to lead with the help of a mortgage expert: 'Find a mortgage with free expert help'. It went live in May 2026, and Ops told us they were getting fewer questions about comparing deals. I don't have an exact number for you, though.

We also tweaked the wording to mention the acquisition. At first we couldn't say Habito by Monzo publicly, so we just had the Habito logo, without 'by Monzo'. Later we could, and to be honest, we tested the copy quite a lot.

Learning number two: people didn't want to leave Monzo. We couldn't avoid sending them to Habito, or to another partner, yet. You had to create a Habito account from the Monzo app, so to make that feel less negative, I proposed framing the account as a benefit: you can check your progress here, in the app, or on Habito's website, wherever suits you.

Then another operational trade-off: Habito needed to know if you were remortgaging with someone else, to add them as a second applicant. We were working on moving those questions into the application form, out of the onboarding, but that was going to take a bit longer.

And from there, we took you to Habito.

11

No design files at Habito, so I became the customer

This one was tricky. Habito had no design team and no design files. And there was no staging environment we could use.

So I applied for a mortgage through Habito myself, as a real customer, and mapped the whole journey. Every screen, email and document could be followed in my design file.

That map helped us design the onboarding, the tracker and everything after it. It showed us what we had to plan for, like the documents that had to go with each recommendation. And it showed gaps, like recommendation notes that didn't say much.

It also gave the whole team one picture of the experience that everyone could follow.

For me, it's simple: I'll do whatever it takes to understand the experience, so we know exactly where the customer is.

12

We shipped the simplest tracker first

Now, the first progress tracker. We actually designed it for Better, and launched it at the same time as the Habito onboarding, so it worked for both partners. I want to show you how it evolved, because the trade-offs say more than the final screen.

Remember how it worked: after the onboarding, customers went straight to the broker's site and filled in the fact-find there. Only once that fact-find was done did we show this tracker in Monzo.

The first iteration was, to me, the ideal one at that point. Everything in one place: your steps, the deals you got, and your expert. It answered 'tell me what's happening' best, and you didn't have to leave the screen to know where you were.

In this version, you only saw the steps ahead of you. To see the ones you'd already finished, you tapped 'Show all'.

But it didn't work for us, because at that point it depended on data we couldn't get. When we sat down with engineering, not every step the broker gave us could be tracked on our side, and there were bits of data we just couldn't get yet.

So with what I learned from engineering, I made a second proposal. We already knew we couldn't show the recommendations or the chat in the app yet, so I took them out and kept only the steps. I proposed leading with the name of the step in the title, with an illustration to go with it, so you always knew where you were. But from an engineering point of view, that meant a new screen for every state, and it would have made the build much longer.

So we shipped the third one: the broker's own steps, with one highlight card at the top. The highlight card was much easier to build, and we could make its title and illustration change with each step. It also had a button to take you back to the broker you were working with, at the step you were on. We chose this version because we could launch it on time, and we saved the ideal version for later.

One more limit: in this first version, we couldn't say 'Barclays approved your mortgage'. We only knew that a lender had approved it, so we could show the step, but not name the lender.

We heard from customers that it was useful, but we didn't see a measurable change in how many people reached or accepted a recommendation. Looking back, I can see why.

First, it depended on data from outside Monzo. If Habito or the lender didn't send us reliable updates, the tracker could go out of date and lose people's trust.

Second, it didn't say who had to act next: you, the broker or the lender. 'The lender approved your mortgage' tells you what happened, but you don't know if you need to do something or just wait.

Third, it ended in a handoff. 'Go to Habito' took people out of Monzo just when they needed to carry on with the broker.

And it didn't help when something went wrong. If a document was missing, or the lender asked for something new, a progress line wasn't enough. People needed to understand the problem and how to fix it.

It was also hard to find. It only showed up in Monzo after people had filled in the fact-find on the broker's site. By then they were used to going there, and they had no clear reason to come back to Monzo to check.

On its own, a tracker mostly reduces the stress of waiting. But it was a point in favour of our vision, and it shaped the next tracker you'll see later.

On the tech side, it was a very hard project. Our engineers had to build a lot of bridges and APIs so the tracker was accurate. At launch, statuses reached Monzo two days late, for both Better and Habito, and we had a few complaints about that. We never fixed it with Better. With Habito, we kept working on the connection, and after a couple of months the tracker in Monzo matched their statuses.

This is a decision I'd make again: ship small. It moved us closer to the vision, and it showed us early how hard the data part was.

You'll see the next version of the tracker in a few minutes.

13

Move the application into Monzo, pre-filled

In parallel, we also moved the form into Monzo. It launched at the end of September 2026. It was the most fragmented part of the whole journey: it was on the broker's website, and it was long. Around 200 questions.

With Better, in 2025, almost half of the people we sent to them never finished the form.

I designed the application inside Monzo, and there are two decisions I care about.

The first is pre-fill. You don't type everything, you just confirm what Monzo already knows about you. That's the answer to 'use what you already know about me'. On the partner's side, pre-fill has been on for everyone since May 2026. Our own form came in September.

The second is saving your progress, so you can come back later, because nobody has all their mortgage details ready in one go. We couldn't do that in the first version.

Habito's own form could save a draft, because they owned the form and the backend. To do it in Monzo, we needed more pieces: a safe place to keep a half-finished application, a way to pick it up when you came back, and an agreed way for Monzo and Habito to share where you were. With sensitive financial data, the interface alone couldn't solve that. So saving progress comes in the next version.

And we measure it step by step, so the team can see exactly where people stop. On the right you can see two versions.

The first one is a simple form, and it went live at the end of September as a 50/50 test: half of our customers got the form in Monzo, and the other half kept the existing journey on Habito's website. We kept it simple on purpose. The main question was: if we bring the same form into Monzo, will more people finish it? If we changed where the form lived, its structure and its content all at once, we wouldn't know what made the difference.

It also let us check, with less risk, that the journey and the connection with Habito worked. This is personal financial information, so we wanted that to be solid before adding more.

In the first read, about 40 percent of the people who started the form in Monzo finished it, compared with about 29 percent on the existing journey. That's roughly a 40 percent relative lift, or about 11 percentage points. The sample was still small, so I treated it as an early directional signal. But it gave us a reason to keep going.

The next version is designed, on the right. The idea is to go from 'fill in this form' to 'check what we already know, and finish the rest in parts'. It tells you what you'll need and how long it takes, so there are fewer surprises. It shows what we've already answered for you, so you type less. And it breaks the form into five sections that keep your progress, so a long task feels manageable.

We expect it to help more people finish, but we still need to test it. And if a pre-filled answer is wrong, people lose trust, so getting the data right matters.

14

Referrals beat the plan, the later stages didn’t

Back to the numbers. In early 2026, this is where we were. This chart only shows the Habito funnel, not the total across partners I showed you before, against its own 2026 targets. Each bar is the percentage of that stage's target, not a conversion rate.

Habito referrals, the people we sent to Habito, were at 153 percent of target.

Reaching a recommendation, meaning they filled in the form and got one, was at 72 percent of its target. Higher, but still behind.

And accepted recommendations, going ahead with one lender and moving on to the next steps, were at only 44 percent of their target.

And that's when people should be most motivated. They'd filled in the whole form and talked to a mortgage expert. Overall, only 7 percent of referrals made it to an accepted recommendation.

We only make money when the mortgage completes. A completed mortgage usually came months after the referral, and in the longest cases up to six months later. So we chose accepted recommendations as an earlier signal.

So the question changed. It wasn't 'how do we send more people?' anymore. It was 'why don't people with a recommendation go ahead?' So I went to find out, with the team.

15

Where people got stuck, and who could fix it

With our PM, I co-led a workshop about the drop-off. Then I turned what we learned into this: an opportunity solution tree.

It's simple. One goal at the top: more people accept their recommendation. Under it, the problem. And under that, the possible causes.

Our hypothesis was that these people really wanted to remortgage, but when they saw the recommendation, they didn't feel confident enough to say yes.

We identified five possible causes. First, relevance: the recommendation felt generic. There was a mortgage expert and a reason behind every recommendation, but customers couldn't see them clearly in the experience.

Second, leaving Monzo: people got sent out of the app. One customer said it felt like being passed on to another company.

Third, timing: some people were still looking around when the recommendation arrived. Fourth, momentum: people were ready, but our follow-up was slow and easy to miss. In one review, 54 percent of people didn't reply after they got their recommendation.

And friction: the form was long, and hard to come back to. We already knew that was a problem we had to solve, and we were actively working on it. That's the form you saw a moment ago.

For each cause, I asked one question: can Monzo fix this, or is it up to Habito? Relevance, for example, was split. Habito decided the recommendation and its reasons. We could improve how those reasons, and the mortgage expert, showed up in Monzo.

And these were only hypotheses. Rates, eligibility, or how fast the mortgage expert replied could also explain part of the drop, and most of that was on Habito's side.

This showed us where design in Monzo could make the biggest difference. What I'll show you next comes from here.

16

A push the moment the recommendation is ready

Now, the moment people decide. The next three slides show one thing: we knew where we wanted to get, but we had to split the work into phases. At that point, the recommendation lived in Habito, not in Monzo. The follow-up was the easiest gap to fix. When the recommendation was ready, we relied on an email from Habito. Monzo didn't send anything.

So I designed a push from Monzo. We sent it on day zero, the moment the recommendation is ready, because that's when people care the most. And again on day three.

Anything that goes outside the app has to be worded very carefully. So the push couldn't mention a rate or a lender. That was the trade-off to stay compliant.

Then, where should the push take you? I designed two options. Option A was a new screen that set expectations: your recommendation is ready, Habito is regulated, and it's free. Option B took you to the progress tracker, and from there to Habito, where your recommendation was.

We went with B. Remember, people found it hard to come back to the tracker, and this gave them a reason to. And it was a screen we already had, so we could test fast. It launched in May 2026.

There was a third option: sending people straight to Habito. I didn't want that. The push comes from Monzo, so landing on another company's website would make the journey feel even more broken. It can even feel unsafe, because people expect a Monzo notification to open Monzo.

Accepted recommendations went up in our early read, but I don't have the exact uplift. So I'd treat it as a positive early signal, not proof that the push alone caused the change.

17

Recommendations in the app, built in steps

The recommendation in the app was a project of its own. It was built in parallel with everything else, and it needed new APIs and new connections with Habito.

We knew where we wanted to get. The question customers were asking themselves was: do I trust this recommendation enough to go ahead? From research, the same insights behind the opportunity tree, I turned that into six things people need. Understand it. Feel it's for them. See alternatives. See the trade-offs, like two years against five, or staying with your lender. See the real cost. And know what to do next.

The first view-only release came in June 2026, after the push, as a small part of the bigger Habito integration, and engineering set its scope. Monzo could show the data Habito already sent us: the lender, the monthly payment, the rate, the length, the fees and the APRC. The advice, and accepting it, stayed with Habito.

Habito recommends one deal, which makes sense, because that's their advice. But customers wanted to compare, so we showed the other deals as secondary options, under the main recommendation. In an early test, we compared it with a version with only one deal. The version with several deals did better on accepted recommendations, meaning people who went ahead with one lender. It was around a 10 percent relative lift. I'd treat that as a directional signal, not conclusive proof. It helped, but we still didn't reach our goal: from about 400 to 1,000 accepted recommendations a month. So more visibility on its own wasn't enough. Customers needed more.

I scored that screen against the six needs. People could see the deal, but they couldn't really decide. It felt generic, because the mortgage expert and their reasons were there in Habito, but you couldn't see them in Monzo. Habito wrote the advice. Our part was how it showed up in Monzo.

In parallel, I was exploring the full version. In the summer, a low-fidelity version showed the total cost first, and staying with your lender against switching, in pounds. Then a hub with tools, for example to stress-test the deal, which went to a design crit in September. We even tried the mortgage expert's reasons as a voice note, but it was too complex, so we parked it. In both, we were testing if different information changed people's decision.

During October, we finalised the new recommendation. The total cost is the real cost of the deal, but in testing that big number scared people away. Monzo had seen the same in 2019, in a remortgage test with the broker Mojo. So the monthly payment went back on top.

And Habito wasn't comfortable showing several deals with the same weight. They had analysed hundreds of deals for you, and the others weren't equal. So after some conversations we agreed: one main recommendation on top, with all its numbers and a note from the mortgage expert explaining why they chose it. Customers could still open 'Deals we ruled out' to see the alternatives.

It also took time for other reasons: tech constraints, giving Habito time to improve how they explained the recommendation, and agreeing with Compliance what had to be on screen and what could go in the documents we send by email. Let me show you the new recommendation.

18

The recommendation, inside Monzo

So this is the new recommendation. I'll tap through it.

First, you see the recommended deal and what you'd pay each month. 'Why this is your best deal' gives you the mortgage expert's reasons.

'How this deal was calculated' shows the real cost over the whole deal, so you see more than the monthly payment. And one step away, you can see the deals we ruled out.

Before you commit you can see every detail, and you can accept, reject, or chat with your mortgage expert, which opens Habito's chat. And the last screen tells you what happens next.

This version went live in November 2026. I designed these experiences while I was at Monzo. The team implemented and launched some of them after I left.

The early feedback from the team is that it looks great. The live data will show if more people accept. To know if they understood it, I'd ask people to explain in their own words why that deal was chosen, and what it means for them.

19

The tracker shows who has to act next

Now, the progress tracker. It answers 'tell me what's happening' better than the previous version.

A remortgage has around ten stages, and the next step can be yours, the mortgage expert's or the lender's. To design it I worked with Habito, to understand the steps they track and make them clear for our customers.

And then our engineers worked with Habito's engineers to build the bridges between the two systems.

I designed one card that changes during the journey. It's orange when it's your turn, and green when someone else is working on it.

In one of our crits, a designer from the pensions team said they were exploring a progress tracker too. I worked with them to shape a shared pattern that could work for both products.

We also redesigned how people get to the tracker. Before, you went to Homeownership in Monzo, and from there to the tracker. Now, if you're remortgaging, the tracker is your first entry point, because that's your main focus. And from there you can still go back to your Homeownership dashboard.

We also added the bell. Every time you move to a new step, you get a notification, so you always know which step you're in. And if you don't want them, you can turn them off by tapping the bell.

And this is also where the chat with your mortgage expert was going to live, which comes next. The first, simpler tracker went live with the Habito onboarding. This version went live in November 2026, after I'd left.

People I worked with tell me the early signals are very positive, but it's too soon to have results.

20

The chat with your mortgage expert: a first step in the app

Now, how we brought the mortgage expert more into the experience. The vision said people should choose how they talk to a mortgage expert.

But in reality, Habito's mortgage experts worked by chat, in a separate tool. And at that time, the chat opened in Habito, outside Monzo.

Our vision was to have the chat inside Monzo. That's the full design, on the right. But we had two options. Wait about nine months for Monzo's own chat. Or bring Intercom, the chat tool Habito used, into our app as a window. We went with Intercom first, because it was faster.

But a second chat inside Monzo is risky. People could ask their mortgage expert Monzo questions, like how to block their card. Or they could ask Monzo support about their mortgage.

So I also designed guardrails: safe answers we could give people when they ask in the wrong place. You can see them on the right.

We also agreed a kill switch before we started. It's a limit that tells you in advance when to stop. If more than 2 percent of chats ended up in the wrong place, we'd investigate. Above 5 percent, we'd roll back. These were limits we agreed before launch, not results.

So, as a step in between, we launched Habito's Intercom chat inside the app in November 2026. I designed it. You can see it on the left.

Before we moved to the full chat in Monzo, first-time buyers became the team's first goal. That was a business decision. We needed to show that buying Habito had been a good decision, and we know that most Monzo customers are working towards buying their first home.

What if designers could check their screens against the rules before Compliance?

There's one more thing I want to show you. One of the slowest parts of the project was approval. Every screen about rates, deals or recommendations went back and forth with Compliance, many times.

So I asked myself: what if designers could check their screens against the rules before they get to Compliance?

I built a checker myself, with Cursor, an AI coding tool. It works from the documents that set out the rules. And we used it inside Monzo.

You paste your copy or add your Figma screens. The tool marks the lines that could break a rule, and suggests a fix. If something required is missing, like the APRC, an annualised measure of the mortgage's cost over its lifetime, fees included, it tells you. It doesn't make it up.

It doesn't approve anything. Compliance still does that. But the review can focus on the hard decisions. Loans, Overdrafts and Wealth asked to use it too. So I ran a lunch and learn, and set up a Slack channel for feedback.

You can see it running on the right. It's a copy I rebuilt for this case study, with no Monzo data in it.

You asked how I use AI: where it helps, and where it isn't worth it. I use it in four ways.

One, to support my work. Like this checker. I'm not a compliance expert, and remembering every rule and checking my screens against it takes a lot of effort. So I built a tool that does it for me.

Two, to check how I communicate. My typos, my English, and whether something I say is wrong. It also helped me build this presentation, with animations I can't explore in Figma.

Three, for quick variations and summaries. Copy and UI variations, summaries of research, or of a Slack channel full of customer feedback.

And four, to share what I learn. For example, I built a tool with all our research insights, so anyone on the team can ask it questions.

And there are things I don't use it for. AI tends to tell you you're right, so I don't let it make decisions for me. I don't use it to decide the strategy, to replace research, or to find solutions before we understand the problem.

So I use it to take mechanical work off my hands. The judgment and the accountability stay with me. AI helps me explore faster and wider, and every decision stays mine.

What I learned

Looking back, I take four things with me.

One: look past the first number. Referrals beat the plan, but people dropped after the recommendation. So we focused on the recommendation, and kept moving the form into Monzo.

Two: say what it is. People thought we were a comparison site. Leading with the mortgage expert changed what they expected.

Three: know the goal, and ship in steps. The vision didn't change, but we got there in pieces: the push, the view-only screen, then the new recommendation.

And four: work with what your partner has. Habito had no design team, so I went through the journey as a customer. And we shipped first steps, like their chat inside our app.
