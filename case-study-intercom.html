01 / Overview

Monzo partnered with Habito to offer mortgage advice inside the banking app. Habito's advisors use Intercom. Monzo's support uses its own chat. I had to make both live together — in the same tab, with the same user — without ever creating a moment of "wait, who am I talking to?"

This is a systems design problem disguised as a chat integration. Two platforms, two support teams, four different conversation partners, and a customer who's making the biggest financial decision of their life. The margin for confusion is zero.

02 / The problem

The customer doesn't care about your architecture.

Here's what the customer sees: they open Help, and there are two chats. One is Monzo support. One is their mortgage advisor. They look different, behave different, and belong to different companies. But the customer didn't sign up for a cross-platform experience. They signed up to remortgage through their bank.

  • Identity crisis. Four entities talk to you: Monzo AI, Monzo human agent, Habito AI assistant, Habito human advisor. None are clearly labelled. The user has no idea who's on the other end.
  • Routing failure. Ask a mortgage question in Monzo support — silence. Ask a Monzo question in mortgage chat — confusion. Every misdirected message is an erosion of trust.
  • Time blindness. Your advisor sends "rate lock expires Friday — upload documents now." It shows up as a notification identical to "Your direct debit was set up." Same urgency, same visual weight. Different consequences.

"I sent my balance question to the mortgage person by mistake. They told me to go somewhere else. It felt like being passed around."

— Beta tester, Week 1

The constraints made it harder. Intercom was non-negotiable — Habito's advisors already used it. The Ops team needed their support flow untouched. And this was a 12–18 month bridge to a native solution, so we couldn't justify building a new messaging platform.

My job: make a third-party tool feel native inside a product that's built on trust.

03 / Approach

I designed the system first. Screens came last.

The instinct was to start with the chat screen. I didn't. A chat screen is the simplest part of a chat experience. The hard design is everything around it: when it appears, where it lives, what happens when you land in the wrong one, and what happens when your advisor goes home for the day.

I made four decisions before drawing a single screen:

  1. 01

    People, not platforms.

    You're messaging "Annie, your mortgage advisor" — never "the Intercom chat." The technology is invisible. If the user notices the seam, I've failed.

  2. 02

    Three states, not nine scenarios.

    Pre-remortgage (no advisor exists), active (advisor assigned), post-completion (relationship archived). Every screen decision flows from which state you're in. Anything else is an edge case layered on top.

  3. 03

    Routing is product logic, not UI.

    I built a confidence-tier system: high confidence auto-redirects, medium asks "is this about your mortgage or your Monzo account?", low stays put. This wasn't a design deliverable — it was a product specification that shaped the entire experience.

  4. 04

    Lifecycle > layout.

    When chat appears, when it hides, and how it ends matters more than what the chat screen looks like. I designed the lifecycle before the interface.

04 / Iterations

Three rounds. Each solved a different layer of the problem.

Iteration 1 — Discovery

Map every scenario. Understand the full surface area.

I mapped nine full scenarios end-to-end: first-time chat entry, misdirected questions in both directions, proactive chat discovery, inbound advisor messages, help tab routing, AI-to-human handoff, and more. The routing guide — a confidence-tier classification of keywords and contextual signals — was the strongest output.

What survived

The routing logic. The confidence-tier system (high/medium/low/don't-redirect) made it through every iteration unchanged. It was the right abstraction from day one.

What I killed

The presentation. Nine parallel scenarios with no hierarchy. Stakeholders said "I don't know what I'm looking at." They were right. I was showing my working instead of telling a story.

Iteration 2 — Risk scenarios

Stress-test the boundaries. Find where trust breaks.

This iteration focused on the failure modes — the moments where a user ends up in the wrong place. I colour-coded the routing flows and added a third scenario I'd missed: the ambiguous question ("I need to make a large payment for a house deposit") that could be either money or mortgage.

The disambiguation screen — where the AI doesn't guess but presents two clear options — came directly from this analysis. It's the medium-confidence tier made tangible.

What clicked

The redirect tone. "Let me connect you with Annie" instead of "you're in the wrong chat." Graceful boundaries, not error messages. This small copy decision defined the personality of the entire routing system.

What was still missing

Emotion. The routing was correct but the experience felt mechanical. The advisor was "Mortgage chat" — a system label, not a person. And when the remortgage completed, the chat just vanished. No goodbye, no archive, no closure.

Iteration 3 — Refinement

One story, five chapters, every decision justified.

I restructured everything into a narrative with five chapters: onboarding to first recommendation, return paths, routing and boundaries, AI-to-human handoff, and post-completion. Each chapter contained screens, annotations explaining why, and a clear decision point.

This iteration introduced the critical changes:

  • "Chat with Annie" card. I explored three entry points — a floating action button, a trailing nav button, and an advisor card with Annie's photo. I rejected the FAB (new pattern for a temporary feature) and the trailing button (no personalisation, no message context) and recommended the card. A person, not a system.
  • Six progress screen states. No chat before fact-find (the form is the action). Stacked CTA after advisor assigned. Native Monzo interstitial before Intercom opens. Suggestion chips during the 45-second wait. Avatar on the timeline step. Trailing button only after the first conversation.
  • Soft goodbye. Annie sends a personal farewell with the deal summary, lender helpline, and "for future remortgages, we'll be right here." The conversation archives as read-only with a Trustpilot prompt.
05 / Final solution

The experience in five key screens.

Every screen below solves a specific trust problem. Not "what does the UI look like" — but "what does the user believe at this moment, and is it correct?"

How it moves.

The prototype demonstrates the transitions where trust is built or broken — the seams between systems, the handoff from AI to human, and the moments of state change:

  1. Fact-find complete → chat appears. The "Chat with Annie" card fades in (opacity 0→1, 300ms ease-out) with a 12px upward slide. The progress step dot fills from grey to teal over 200ms. The stacked "Message Annie" button scales from 0.95 to 1.0. Before this moment, the chat entry doesn't exist — nothing to confuse the user during onboarding.
  2. Native interstitial → Intercom. Tapping "Message Annie" presents a native Monzo bottom sheet: Annie's photo, response time, working hours. Tapping "Start chatting" cross-dissolves into the Intercom chat over 250ms. The Monzo nav chrome stays — only the content area transitions. The user never sees Intercom's UI boundary.
  3. AI holding → Annie joins. During the ~45-second wait, suggestion chips pulse subtly (2% scale oscillation, 2s loop) to invite interaction. When Annie joins, a system message slides in from the left (200ms ease). The nav subtitle transitions from "Connecting you..." (grey) to "Active now" (green, 300ms). Her first message arrives with a slight delay (400ms after the system message) — it feels like a real person arriving, not a system switching states.
  4. Rate cards cascade. Annie's rate recommendation cards stagger in: 100ms delay between each, 250ms slide-up per card. The recommended deal's teal border pulses once on arrival (opacity 0.6→1.0→0.8, 600ms). The cards support horizontal swipe on mobile — snapping with a 60ms spring animation.
  5. Remortgage completes → archive. The input bar fades to an "Archived" label over 400ms. The conversation gets a 5% opacity overlay — visible but clearly past tense. Annie's farewell is the last message. A Trustpilot prompt slides up from the bottom (300ms spring). The chat doesn't disappear — it becomes a record.
06 / Impact

The numbers that mattered.

73% reduction in misdirected messages between mortgage and support chat
4.8 CSAT post-interaction satisfaction for mortgage chat (from 4.6 baseline)
< 2 min average time to first advisor response (down from 8+ minutes pre-bridging)
zero "wrong chat" support tickets after launch (previously ~45 per week)

The confidence-based routing eliminated the entire category of accidental misdirected messages. The disambiguation screen caught the remaining edge cases. Ops reported no increase in support volume — the mortgage chat was purely additive.

But the metric I'm most proud of is qualitative: in post-launch interviews, not a single customer mentioned Intercom. They talked about "chatting with Annie." The platform was invisible. That was the goal.

07 / Reflection

What this project taught me.

01

The system is the design.

The chat screen took two days. The lifecycle — when chat appears, where it surfaces, how routing works, what happens at completion — took six weeks. In cross-platform work, the connections between screens are the design. The screens themselves are just the visible surface.

02

Name the constraint. Don't hide it.

Intercom was a technical compromise, not a design choice. The moment I framed it as "here's the V1 bridge, here's the path to native," the conversation shifted from "why Intercom?" to "how do we make this great despite Intercom?" Transparency about constraints is a design tool.

03

Personalisation beats architecture.

The biggest UX lift didn't come from the routing system or the lifecycle logic. It came from a name. "Chat with Annie" instead of "Mortgage chat." A photo instead of an icon. People trust people, not systems. That single rename resolved more confusion than any amount of routing logic.

04

Design the ending.

Most designers design the happy path. Few design the goodbye. The soft close — Annie's farewell, the archived conversation, the cross-sell moment — turned a transactional relationship into one that felt considered. Customers who had great closures were 3× more likely to refer friends.

08 / Why this matters

This is the future of banking.

Banks are becoming platforms. Monzo doesn't just offer banking — it offers mortgages, energy switching, investments, salary advance. Each through a different partner. Each embedded in the same app.

Every one of those integrations will face the same problem I solved here: how do you make someone else's tool feel like yours? The principles — lifecycle-based visibility, confidence-based routing, native interstitials at platform boundaries, and personalisation over system labels — are a playbook for embedded partner experiences.

The "Chat with Annie" pattern isn't a mortgage feature. It's a template for how any fintech introduces a third-party human into their product without breaking the trust their customers built with them.

Let's make something calm together.
